# Playbook: documents, citations, and reference material

Covers legal, regulatory, academic, policy, and internal reference material —
anything where the claim is "this document says X" or "this rule requires Y".

## Where the primary source actually is

| Claim about | Primary source | Not the primary source |
|---|---|---|
| What a law or regulation says | The official text, at the version in force on the relevant date | A commentary, a summary, a training deck, your memory |
| What a contract obliges | The signed original, including annexes and amendments | The template it was drafted from, the negotiation email |
| What a court decided | The published decision | The headnote, the press release, a citing article |
| What a study found | The paper, and its actual method and sample | The abstract, the press coverage, a citing paper |
| Internal policy | The current controlled version | An intranet page, a wiki, a slide from last year |
| A deadline | The rule that sets it plus the triggering event's date | Someone's calendar entry |

## The version-and-date problem

For documents, "true" is almost always **true as of a date and a version**. A
statute amended last month, a contract with an unread amendment, a policy
superseded quietly — the text you are reading may be authentic and still wrong
for the question asked.

Every document claim carries three things or it is not `[V]`:

1. **Which version** — edition, amendment number, "as amended through <date>"
2. **In force when** — the date that matters is the date of the *event*, not today
3. **Where exactly** — article, clause, section, page, paragraph

> `[V]` Art. 39, Law 13.709/2018, as in force on 2026-08-15 — text retrieved from
> the official gazette, not from a summary.

## Quotation integrity

Quote, don't paraphrase, when the exact words carry the obligation. A paraphrase
of a legal or contractual clause is an interpretation, and interpretations belong
in your analysis, not in the evidence column.

When you must condense, mark it as `[I]` — your reading of the clause — and put
the clause's locator beside it so the reader can check your reading against the
text.

Never reconstruct a quotation from memory and present it as a quotation. If you
cannot retrieve the text, the claim is `[U]`.

## Citation chains

The dominant failure in document work is **the uninspected citation**: source A
says "per B, X is true", you cite A, and nobody ever opens B. Often B says
something narrower, or B has been superseded, or B does not exist.

Walk the chain to the origin, or mark the claim `[R]` and name the intermediary:

> `[R]` per the compliance memo of 2026-03-02, which attributes this to ANPD
> guidance — I did not retrieve the underlying guidance.

Two specific traps:

**Circular attribution.** A cites B, B cites A. Two documents, one assertion,
zero evidence. Check whether your two "independent" sources are actually one.

**Existence.** Confirm the cited authority exists before relying on it — the
case number resolves, the article number is within the statute's range, the
clause is present in the executed copy rather than only in the draft. A citation
that does not resolve is disproved, which is a strong and cheap finding.

## Absence claims

"The contract does not require X", "there is no clause about Y" — negatives need
a described search, because a negative from a partial reading is worthless:

> `[V]` No confidentiality clause. Searched the full executed PDF (18pp, including
> both annexes) for "confidential", "sigilo", "non-disclosure" — no hits.

State what you searched and how. An unqualified "there is no X" is `[U]`
pretending to be `[V]`.

## Signatures, execution, and effect

A document existing is not the same as a document being in effect. Check
separately, and mark separately:

- Is it **executed** — signed by all parties, dated?
- Is it **complete** — all annexes, schedules, and amendments present?
- Is it **in force** — commencement date reached, conditions precedent met, not
  terminated or superseded?

Any of these can be `[U]` while the others are `[V]`, and the distinction usually
matters more than the text itself.

## Working with translations

A translated text is a derived source. When the obligation turns on precise
wording, cite the original language and mark the translation `[I]`. Where an
official translation exists, say which one — different official translations of
the same instrument do diverge.
